# PROTOCOL 2 — THE PITCH ENGINE
### How to turn a finished manuscript into an agent and a publishing deal, with AI doing the heavy lifting
*Module 2 of the Authority Stack. Begins only after Protocol 1 returns "READY FOR PROTOCOL 2." Reconstructed from the actual proposal and pitch strategy built for "Closing the AI Wage Gap."*

---

## WHO THIS IS FOR AND WHAT IT ASSUMES

Same reader as Protocol 1: smart, expert, not a natural writer, not fluent with AI. This protocol assumes you now have:

- A finished, de-AI-ed manuscript (or at minimum a rock-solid Introduction plus one or two fully written sample chapters, which is what agents actually read first).
- Your `voice.md`, `positioning.md` and `book-blueprint.md` files from Protocol 1.
- Real platform numbers you can look up (newsletter subscribers, LinkedIn followers, podcast downloads, speaking audiences, client logos).

**The core truth of this phase:** publishers and agents do not buy manuscripts, they buy a *concept plus a platform plus a plan*. Your job is to package all three so cleanly that a yes is easy. AI can build 90% of that package. It cannot do the two human things: send warm intros, and be honest about your real numbers.

**A hard rule for this phase:** every claim in your proposal about yourself must be true and checkable. Agents and editors verify. One inflated number ends the conversation. Where you are unsure, mark it and confirm it before it goes out.

---

## THE MAP

```
STEP 1  Choose the path: traditional vs hybrid (decision matrix)
STEP 2  Build the proposal (the one asset that does everything)
STEP 3  Write the sample chapter(s) to submission standard
STEP 4  Build the agent target list (20-30, tiered)
STEP 5  Write the query letter (the 4-paragraph door-opener)
STEP 6  Run the submission campaign (tiered, tracked, timed)
STEP 7  Handle responses: R&R, calls, offers, rejections
STEP 8  (Parallel) The hybrid path, if or when you choose it
STEP 9  Negotiate and choose
```

Realistic timeline: **3-6 months to land an agent, another 3-6 to land a publisher, 12-18 months from deal to shelves.** Roughly 18-30 months total for traditional. Hybrid can be 9-15 months start to shelf. Plan your life and your launch around those numbers, not around hope.

# STEP 1 — CHOOSE THE PATH: TRADITIONAL VS HYBRID

**Why:** These are two different games with different economics, timelines and prestige. Decide before you spend a day pitching, because it changes who you contact and how.

**The honest comparison:**

| | Traditional | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|
| **You pay** | Nothing; you get an advance | ~$20,000-$60,000 upfront |
| **Royalties** | Lower (roughly 10-15% list) | Higher (roughly 50-70% of net) |
| **Timeline to shelf** | 18-30 months from now | 9-15 months from contract |
| **Control** | Less | More |
| **Prestige / distribution** | Highest (Big Five, retail scale) | Good, but not Big-Five retail scale |
| **What they demand** | Differentiated concept + strong platform | Platform + budget tolerance + ability to hand-sell |
| **Best when** | Proposal and platform are strong, you can wait | You want it out by a date and see it as a business/platform asset with clear ROI |

Top business-focused hybrids to research: **Greenleaf Book Group, Page Two, Amplify/Ideapress-style presses, BenBella's hybrid imprints.** (Always verify current models; they change.)

> **COPY-PASTE — decision helper:**
> ```
> Read positioning.md and book-blueprint.md. Here are my real constraints: [target pub date],
> [budget I could invest if hybrid], [my platform numbers], [how much control matters to me],
> [why I'm writing this book: pure sales / lead-gen for my business / legacy]. Given all of it,
> lay out the case for traditional vs hybrid FOR MY situation specifically, name the 3 questions
> I must answer to decide, and give me a recommended primary path plus a fallback. Be blunt.
> ```

**The sensible default (what the AI Wage Gap plan chose):** spend 3-4 months finishing the proposal and sample chapters, **pursue traditional first** (top agents) for 3-6 months, and if interest is weak or misaligned, **shift to evaluating 2-4 top hybrid offers.** This preserves both options until the market tells you where the best partnership is. You do not have to decide forever today. You decide where to start.

# STEP 2 — BUILD THE PROPOSAL (the asset that does everything)

**Why:** The book proposal is the single document that gets you an agent, gets the agent a publisher, and forces you to sharpen your own thinking. For nonfiction, the proposal sells the book, not the finished manuscript. Build it once, well, and reuse it everywhere.

**The standard nonfiction proposal has these sections, in this order:**
1. Overview / Why this book (the hook)
2. Target audience
3. Market need and timing (Why now)
4. Competitive titles and positioning
5. About the author (platform and credentials)
6. Detailed Table of Contents
7. Sample chapter descriptions
8. Marketing and promotion plan
9. One or two full sample chapters

Build it section by section. Do not try to draft the whole thing in one prompt.

## Step 2.1 — The Overview / hook

**Why:** An agent decides in the first paragraph whether to keep reading. This section makes the enemy visceral and the book inevitable.

> **COPY-PASTE:**
> ```
> Using positioning.md, book-blueprint.md and research.md, write the OVERVIEW section of my book
> proposal (600-900 words). Structure: open by making the enemy visceral with a concrete scene or
> the billboard number; name and define the problem in my own coined term; show the 3-4 ways it
> shows up in my reader's real life; explain why every existing solution is fragmented; then state
> the 3 things this book does that no existing book does. End on the promise. My voice per voice.md.
> No hype words. Every stat carries a [SOURCE?] tag for me to verify.
> ```

## Step 2.2 — Audience, market and timing

> **COPY-PASTE:**
> ```
> Draft three proposal sections from positioning.md and research.md:
> 1. TARGET AUDIENCE: primary ICP (age, geography, roles, situation, constraints) and 2-3
>    secondary audiences. Be specific enough that an agent pictures a real person.
> 2. MARKET NEED AND TIMING: the reader's pain points, then the data backdrop (real cited
>    figures, [SOURCE?] each), then a tight "why this book now" argument.
> 3. Keep it evidence-led, not adjective-led. My voice.
> ```

## Step 2.3 — Competitive titles and positioning

**Why:** This proves you know the field and gives the agent the comp language they will use to sell you internally. You already built most of this in Protocol 1, Phase 1.3.

> **COPY-PASTE:**
> ```
> Using my comps analysis from research.md, write the COMPETITIVE TITLES AND POSITIONING section.
> For each of 4-6 real comparable books: title, author, year, its focus, its genuine strength, and
> the specific gap it leaves that my book fills. Then a positioning statement ("[My book] combines
> the [X] of [comp] with the [Y] of [comp], but for [my underserved reader]") and a bulleted
> "unique value" list. Only real, verifiable books; mark any uncertain one [VERIFY].
> ```

## Step 2.4 — About the author (platform)

**Why:** After the concept, platform is what publishers weigh most. This section must be true, specific and quantified.

> **COPY-PASTE:**
> ```
> Draft the ABOUT THE AUTHOR section from positioning.md. Lead with the credential that matters
> most to THIS book, then prior books/media, then the reach-to-the-exact-audience proof. Leave
> clearly marked blanks for me to insert hard numbers: [newsletter subscribers + growth trend],
> [LinkedIn followers + avg post reach], [podcast downloads], [speaking audience size/frequency],
> [client logos]. Write it as quiet proof, not bragging. Third person. My voice.
> ```

**Then fill the blanks with real numbers.** Look each one up. Do not estimate upward. A publisher will check.

## Step 2.5 — TOC, chapter descriptions and marketing plan

> **COPY-PASTE:**
> ```
> From book-blueprint.md and chapter-skeletons.md, draft:
> 1. The DETAILED TABLE OF CONTENTS (Parts and chapters with final titles).
> 2. SAMPLE CHAPTER DESCRIPTIONS: 1-2 tight paragraphs per chapter covering the problem it solves,
>    the frameworks/tools it introduces, and the concrete output the reader ends with.
> 3. A MARKETING AND PROMOTION PLAN: my existing channels (newsletter, LinkedIn, podcast, speaking),
>    planned launch activities (conference talks, webinars, corporate workshops based on the OS),
>    media angles (op-eds/excerpts and the outlets that fit), and community/course tie-ins. Leave
>    blanks for hard reach numbers.
> ```

**QC check:** Read the full proposal as a busy agent would, skimming. Does the first paragraph hook? Are the numbers real? Is the differentiation obvious by page 2? If not, sharpen before you send.

# STEP 3 — WRITE THE SAMPLE CHAPTER(S) TO SUBMISSION STANDARD

**Why:** Agents want to see you can actually write, not just outline. Submit one or two fully written, fully polished chapters, ideally Chapter 1 (the hook) or the chapter with your signature framework (for the AI Wage Gap book, the "Calculate Your Score" chapter). You already produced these in Protocol 1. Here you make sure they are submission-clean.

> **COPY-PASTE:**
> ```
> I'm submitting Chapter [N] as a proposal sample. Run a final agent-readiness pass: is the hook
> strong enough to survive a skim? Is the framework unmistakably proprietary and useful? Is the
> voice consistent and human (no AI tells)? Would this chapter make an agent want the rest? Give me
> the 5 highest-leverage line-level fixes, then apply the ones I approve. Confirm QuillBot
> plagiarism is clean and AI-detection is in human range before I send.
> ```

**QC check:** The sample chapter is your audition. It should be the best-edited thing in the whole package.

# STEP 4 — BUILD THE AGENT TARGET LIST (20-30, tiered)

**Why:** Querying randomly wastes your best shot. You target agents who have recently sold books like yours to houses you want, in two tiers so you can test and refine your pitch on tier 2 before burning tier 1... or lead with tier 1 while your pitch is sharpest. (The plan's guidance: query 5-10 top agents first, expand to the next 10-15 with a refined pitch if there is little movement after 4-6 weeks.)

> **COPY-PASTE:**
> ```
> Help me build a target list of literary agents for a business/careers/AI book like mine
> (positioning.md). For each agent I should research, tell me exactly what to look for and where:
> 1. Agents who have sold comparable titles (business, careers, future-of-work, AI-and-work) to
>    major houses in the last ~3 years. Explain how to find them via the comps' acknowledgments
>    pages and agency sites and Publishers Marketplace.
> 2. What signals a good fit (their recent sales, their stated interests, their submission rules).
> 3. What DISQUALIFIES an agent for me (wrong genre, closed to queries, no recent relevant sales).
> Give me a research checklist and a tracking-table template with columns: agent, agency, why-fit,
> tier (1/2), recent relevant sale, submission rules, warm-intro path, status, date sent, response.
> ```

**Then do the human research.** The AI can tell you *how* to build the list and *what* to look for. You (or the AI via live web search) confirm each agent is real, currently open and a genuine fit. **Never trust an AI-generated agent name or email without verifying it exists.** Fabricated agents are a classic failure mode.

> **COPY-PASTE (verification):**
> ```
> For each agent on my list, give me the exact searches to confirm: (a) they are a real, currently
> active agent, (b) they are open to queries now, (c) their exact submission requirements (query
> only? query + proposal? first N pages?), (d) whether they sold anything close to my book recently.
> Flag any I should drop. I will not query anyone unverified.
> ```

# STEP 5 — WRITE THE QUERY LETTER (the 4-paragraph door-opener)

**Why:** The query is the single most important short document you will write. It is 3-4 paragraphs and it decides whether the agent opens your proposal. It is customized per agent.

**The proven structure:**
1. **The hook** (make them feel the enemy: the AI Wage Gap problem in a sentence or two).
2. **Why this book now** (timing and the gap no existing book fills).
3. **Why you** (platform and background, in one tight paragraph with real reach numbers).
4. **Comps + close** (1-2 comparable titles, how yours differs, and a line noting the full proposal and sample chapters are ready).

> **COPY-PASTE — base query:**
> ```
> Write my base query letter (250-350 words) to a literary agent, in my voice. Four paragraphs:
> (1) HOOK that makes the agent feel the AI Wage Gap problem viscerally; (2) WHY NOW: the timing
> and the gap in existing books; (3) WHY ME: platform + background in one paragraph, with blanks
> for my real reach numbers ([X subscribers], [speak to Y/year], [coach Z executives]); (4) COMPS
> and close: 1-2 real comps, my one-line differentiation, and a line that the full proposal and
> two sample chapters are ready on request. Warm, confident, specific. No hype words, no groveling.
> ```

> **COPY-PASTE — per-agent customization (run for each):**
> ```
> Customize my base query for [Agent Name] at [Agency]. Reference one or two relevant books they
> sold, explain in one line how my book complements rather than competes with their list, and match
> their stated submission rules ([paste the rules]). Keep it under 350 words. Do not fabricate any
> detail about them; if I haven't given you a real fact about their list, leave a [confirm] blank.
> ```

**QC check:** The best queries feel like they were written only for that agent. If you could swap the agent's name and nothing else would change, it is not customized enough. And warm beats cold: if you know another author, an editor, a journalist or a conference host who can make a soft intro, that can double your response rate. Ask the AI to draft the intro-request note, but you send it.

> **COPY-PASTE — warm intro request:**
> ```
> Draft a short, low-pressure note I can send to [name] asking for a soft intro to [Agent], for my
> book. Make it easy for them to say yes or no, give them a one-line description they can forward,
> and make clear I'll do all the follow-up work. My voice, warm, brief.
> ```

# STEP 6 — RUN THE SUBMISSION CAMPAIGN (tiered, tracked, timed)

**Why:** This is a pipeline, not a lottery ticket. You run it like outbound sales: batch, track, learn, refine.

**The cadence:**
- **Weeks 0-2:** Query tier 1 (your top 5-10 agents), each customized. Log every send in your tracker.
- **Weeks 2-6:** Wait. Most agents respond in 4-8 weeks; many not at all (no response is a soft no). Do not nudge before 4 weeks.
- **Week 6:** If little or no positive movement, this is data. Refine the pitch before tier 2.

> **COPY-PASTE — mid-campaign diagnosis:**
> ```
> I queried these [N] tier-1 agents [paste tracker: who, when, response]. I got [X requests, Y
> rejections, Z silence]. Diagnose: is the concept, the query, the comps, or the platform the weak
> link? Give me the single highest-leverage change to the query or proposal before I send tier 2,
> and rewrite the affected section.
> ```

- **Weeks 6-10:** Send tier 2 with the refined pitch.
- **Throughout:** Keep running the Phase 4 interview/podcast loop from Protocol 1. A growing platform strengthens every week you are in submission.

**QC check:** If tier 1 produces zero manuscript requests, stop and fix the package. Do not burn all 30 agents on a pitch that is not landing. The point of tiering is to learn cheaply.

# STEP 7 — HANDLE RESPONSES

**Why:** Requests, calls, "revise and resubmit" (R&R) and offers each need a specific move. Rejections are the default and mean little individually.

> **COPY-PASTE — manuscript/proposal request:**
> ```
> An agent requested my full proposal and samples. Draft a warm, professional 3-line reply that
> sends them cleanly (proposal + 2 sample chapters), thanks them, and asks their preferred timeline
> without pressure. My voice.
> ```

> **COPY-PASTE — revise and resubmit:**
> ```
> An agent passed but invited a revise-and-resubmit with this feedback: [paste feedback]. Separate
> it into: (a) changes that genuinely strengthen the book and I should make, (b) changes that fight
> my thesis or voice and I should politely decline, (c) things to clarify. Draft my reply and a
> revision plan. I am the author; the book stays mine.
> ```

> **COPY-PASTE — call prep:**
> ```
> I have a call with an interested agent. Give me: the 8 questions I must ask (their vision,
> editorial involvement, submission strategy, houses they'd target, timeline, comms style, terms,
> their recent track record with books like mine), the 5 questions they'll likely ask me and strong
> answers in my voice, and 3 red flags that should make me walk. Keep it tight.
> ```

**On rejections:** most queries get rejected or ignored. That is the base rate, not a verdict. You only need one yes. Keep the pipeline moving.

# STEP 8 — THE HYBRID PATH (parallel or fallback)

**Why:** If traditional is slow or misaligned, a top hybrid gets a quality book to market faster with far higher royalties, in exchange for your capital and your willingness to hand-sell. Run this in parallel from the start if speed-to-market matters more than Big-Five prestige.

> **COPY-PASTE — hybrid shortlist and diligence:**
> ```
> I'm evaluating hybrid publishers for a business book. Help me build a shortlist (Greenleaf, Page
> Two, Ideapress/Amplify-style, BenBella hybrid imprints, others you can verify) and, for each, the
> exact diligence questions to ask: precise services included (developmental edit? launch campaign?
> long-tail support?), print quality and distribution channels (Ingram? retail placement?), royalty
> split and total upfront cost, and 2-3 comparable business books they published and how those did.
> Give me a scoring rubric to compare offers on cost, services, distribution and track record.
> ```

**How to pitch a hybrid well:** lead with platform and business use cases (corporate speaking, consulting, course tie-ins, your ability to move copies directly), bring concrete sales goals (for example 5,000-10,000 copies in year one), and be clear on your budget tolerance. Hybrids care most about your ability to hand-sell.

> **COPY-PASTE — hybrid pitch:**
> ```
> Draft my outreach to a hybrid publisher: a short intro that leads with platform and hand-sell
> ability, states my concept in two lines, names my target pub date and rough sales goal, and asks
> for their services menu and economics. Attach-ready proposal already exists. My voice, business-
> like, confident.
> ```

**QC check:** Never sign a hybrid deal without answers to the diligence questions and without seeing how two of their comparable titles actually performed. A hybrid that only takes your money and prints the book is a printer, not a publisher.

# STEP 9 — NEGOTIATE AND CHOOSE

**Why:** Whether it is an agent agreement, a traditional offer via your agent, or competing hybrid offers, you want to compare on the real terms, not the flattery.

> **COPY-PASTE:**
> ```
> Here are my options: [paste each offer: type, financial terms, timeline, services, control,
> distribution, track record]. Build a side-by-side comparison on the axes that matter for MY goals
> from positioning.md (income vs platform vs prestige vs speed vs control). Flag the 3 terms in each
> that I should push back on, and give me the language to negotiate each. Then give me your
> recommendation and the one question I still need answered before I sign. This is not legal advice;
> I will have counsel review any contract.
> ```

**The two things AI must never do here:** it does not sign, and it does not replace a publishing lawyer or a real agent's judgment on contract terms. Use it to prepare and compare. Use humans to close.

## APPENDIX B — THE HONESTY GATE (read before every send)

Before any query, proposal or hybrid pitch leaves your outbox, confirm:
1. Every platform number is real and current (you looked it up).
2. Every comp is a real book you can name and describe.
3. Every agent detail is verified (real person, open, right genre).
4. No fabricated quote, sale, or credential anywhere.
5. The sample chapter is your best-edited work and passes QuillBot clean.

Agents and editors verify. Your credibility is the whole asset. Protect it.
